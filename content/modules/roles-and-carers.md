body: |-
  Asked what government is, the Analects answers with relations and not
  with a principle. There is government, the passage runs, when the prince
  is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and
  the son is son.[^1] No rule is stated. What is stated is that things go
  well when each party to a relation is actually doing what that relation
  asks, and badly when they are not.

  The duke who asked the question answers it himself, and his answer is
  the part worth keeping. Good, he says; if, indeed, the prince be not
  prince, the minister not minister, the father not father, and the son
  not son, although I have my revenue, can I enjoy it?[^1] The revenue
  still arrives. The grain is in the store and the soldiers are paid.
  What the duke has noticed is that the thing the revenue was for has
  gone, and that no quantity of it restores what is missing. The test is
  not whether the outputs are produced. It is whether anybody is still
  standing where they were supposed to stand.

  This is why the Ames and Rosemont reading calls it role ethics rather
  than virtue ethics. The subject of the sentence is never a bare
  individual with qualities. It is somebody standing in a relation to
  somebody else, and the obligations run along the relation rather than
  out from the person.

  Now put a machine in one of the roles. A care robot lifts, reminds,
  monitors, talks, and is there at four in the morning when no daughter
  is. Measured on outcomes it may be winning: fewer falls, better
  medication adherence, less loneliness by any scale you can administer.
  Measured on the Analects it is not obviously doing anything at all,
  because what the passage asks is that the son be son, and no amount of
  the son's functions performed by something else makes that true. This
  is the duke's revenue again, in a nursing home.

  The honest counter is that the relation may be the parent's, not the
  child's, to lose. If the mother is attended, spoken to and known, and
  experiences herself as cared for, a tradition that still finds something
  missing owes an account of who exactly is worse off. Role ethics has
  one: the obligation was constitutive of the child, so the loss is in the
  person who no longer stands where they stood.

  Book 13 adds the part that bites in procurement. If names are not
  correct, it says, language does not accord with the truth of things.[^2]
  A device sold as a companion, a carer, a friend, is a name claiming a
  relation. Where the name is accepted, the duties that used to attach to
  the relation quietly attach to nobody, because the thing now holding the
  title cannot bear them.

  The cost of this view is the one its critics never let it forget. The
  passage that opens this module names two relations, prince to minister
  and father to son, and both are unequal; the paper Confucian
  Role-Ethics with Non-Domination concedes that role ethics is too ready
  to authorise hierarchy. Li-Hsiang Lisa Rosenlee's Confucianism and
  Women traces what that cost, as filial piety and patrilineal descent
  held women in place for centuries.

  Applied to care, that is not an abstraction. The daughter in this
  module is a daughter and not a son for a reason, and the duty the robot
  is accused of displacing has historically been extracted from women who
  were given no say in it. A tradition that says the robot breaks something has to answer
  whether what it breaks was fairly imposed, and whether a machine that
  lifts the duty off one daughter is destroying a relation or ending an
  extraction. The reconstruction needs a principle role ethics does not
  contain, which is the non-domination the paper proposes and the
  Analects never states.
