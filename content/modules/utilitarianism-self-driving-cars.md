Bentham's utilitarianism starts from a claim that sounds almost administrative: pleasure and pain can be measured, and the right act is whichever produces the largest net sum of pleasure over pain, added across everyone affected. In the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), Chapter IV, he lists the variables: "its intensity," "its duration," "its certainty or uncertainty," "its propinquity or remoteness" and, when weighing an act's effect on a community, "its extent, i.e. the number of persons to whom it extends." Sum the pleasures, sum the pains, subtract. The theory does not distinguish whose pleasure or pain is counted, a unit is a unit.

Mill kept the sum but rejected Bentham's assumption that pleasures differ only in amount. In Utilitarianism (1861), Chapter 2, he argues some pleasures are "more desirable and more valuable than others" independent of quantity, and that competent judges who have experienced both a higher and lower pleasure will reliably prefer the higher one. His line: "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." Mill's utilitarianism still aggregates. It just aggregates a ranked good, not a flat one.

Self-driving cars gave the tradition an unusually literal test case. In 2016, MIT researchers built the Moral Machine — an online platform showing millions of users the trolley problem reframed as an unavoidable crash: swerve and kill one pedestrian, continue and kill five, spare the young or the passengers or the pedestrians. Awad et al. published the results in Nature in 2018: 40 million decisions from 233 countries, showing broad but not universal preference for minimizing deaths and sparing children, with real cross-cultural variation underneath. The paper reads as an empirical map of what a Benthamite calculus would need to encode if built from popular preference.

Germany's government-appointed Ethics Commission on Automated and Connected Driving took a harder line in its June 2017 report. Its proposed Rule 9 states that in an unavoidable accident, "any distinction based on personal features (age, gender, physical or mental constitution) is strictly prohibited" and that "it is also prohibited to offset victims against one another," a direct rejection of Bentham-style aggregation. When Germany passed its Act on Autonomous Driving in 2021, only half of Rule 9 became binding law. Section 1e(2) of the amended Road Traffic Act requires accident-avoidance systems that ignore personal characteristics like age or gender. As legal scholar Lando Kirchmair notes, the law contains no general prohibition on offsetting victims against each other. Germany became the first country with a statutory rule on autonomous-vehicle crash behavior. The harder Benthamite question, whether an algorithm may trade fewer lives for more, stayed unresolved even though the government's own commission had taken a position on it.

Rawls gives the philosophical version of that rejected half of Rule 9. In A Theory of Justice (1971), he writes that utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons," because it treats society's total welfare the way one person treats their own trade-offs across a lifetime, as if gains and losses could simply be netted out. A person can rationally accept pain now for pleasure later because it is the same person bearing both. Two different people are not the same subject — Rawls's point is that summing their welfare erases a distinction utilitarianism has no principled way to notice. The pedestrian who dies so five passengers live is not compensated by their survival. Rawls would say the calculus was never entitled to net the two together, close to the position German lawmakers stopped short of writing into law.
