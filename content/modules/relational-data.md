body: |-
  Most data rules start from a person who owns facts about themselves and
  may trade them. Consent, portability, deletion, the right to be
  forgotten: each assumes a bounded individual holding a bundle of
  attributes. Sabelo Mhlambi's argument is that this starting point is the
  problem, not the solution. Personhood, he writes, is fundamentally
  relational.[^2]

  Take that seriously and a record stops being a possession. If what makes
  you a person is the set of relationships you stand in, then a file about
  you is a file about those relationships, and the people on the other end
  of them did not consent to anything. Your medical history is your
  family's genetics. Your location trace is everyone you regularly meet.
  Your messages are somebody else's words.

  Mhlambi's sharpest line is about what computation does to the idea of
  privacy itself: the notion that truths, and perhaps all truths, about an
  individual can be rationally computed destroys the core idea of
  privacy.[^1] The damage is not that a particular secret escaped. It is
  that a system which can infer the unshared has made the distinction
  between shared and unshared stop meaning anything. You cannot withhold
  what can be derived.

  Thaddeus Metz gives the underlying claim its most compact form: moral
  value fundamentally lies not in the individual, but rather in a
  relationship between individuals.[^3] On that view a data protection
  regime built entirely around individual rights is aiming at the wrong
  object. It will defend the record and miss the relationship.

  What this changes in practice is the question asked at design time. Not
  whether this person agreed, but what the system does to the ties the
  person is held in: whether it exposes people who were never asked,
  whether it lets a community see what is being inferred about it, and
  whether anybody can contest a classification that describes a group
  rather than a member.

  The weakness is in the word community. Relational language is at its
  most persuasive and least precise exactly here. Somebody has to decide
  what the community wants, who speaks for it, and who is inside it. Those
  are the questions a government answers for you if you do not answer them
  yourself, and Bernard Matolino and Wenceslaus Kwindingwi's objection to
  Ubuntu as a public ethic is that it has repeatedly been answered by
  whoever already held power.

  So the module ends where it should be hardest to agree. A data regime
  built on relationships, with no account of who speaks for the
  relationship, hands the collective interest to whoever is loudest. The
  individual rights this view finds too thin are also the thing a
  dissenter holds when the community has decided against them. The
  reconstruction worth having keeps both, and nothing in the tradition
  guarantees that it can.
