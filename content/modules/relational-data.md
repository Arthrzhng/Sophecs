body: |-
  Most data rules start from a person who owns facts about themselves and
  may trade them. Consent, portability, deletion, the right to be
  forgotten: each assumes a bounded individual holding a bundle of
  attributes, deciding which to hand over and on what terms. Sabelo
  Mhlambi's argument is that this starting point is the problem rather
  than the solution, and he puts it in four words. Personhood, he writes,
  is fundamentally relational.[^2]

  The second sentence is the one that does the damage. Writing about
  digital colonialism, surveillance capitalism, and models that generalise
  from aggregated personal data, Mhlambi says that the notion that truths,
  and perhaps all truths, about an individual can be rationally computed
  destroys the core idea of privacy.[^1] Read the two together and the
  claim is not that computation invades privacy. It is that computation of
  that kind leaves nothing for privacy to be about.

  Hold that against the standard model and the machinery starts to look
  beside the point. Consent governs disclosure, and the problem is
  inference. Deletion removes a record, and the problem is that the record
  was never the thing: a model that has learned the shape of people like
  you can reconstitute the deleted. The right to withhold assumes there is
  something only you can supply. Where enough has been aggregated, there
  is not.

  The relational half explains why that matters more than an ordinary
  privacy loss. If what makes you a person is the set of relationships you
  stand in, then being computable is not only exposure. It is a claim that
  the relationships are surplus to the description, that you can be set
  out in full without them.

  So a record stops being a possession. A file about you is a file about
  the people you stand in relation to, and they did not consent to
  anything. Your medical history is your family's genetics. Your location
  trace is everyone you regularly meet. Your messages are somebody else's
  words. Thaddeus Metz gives the underlying claim its most compact form:
  moral value fundamentally lies not in the individual, but rather in a
  relationship between individuals.[^3] On that view a protection regime
  built entirely around individual rights is aiming at the wrong object.
  It will defend the record and miss the relationship.

  What this changes in practice is the question asked at design time. Not
  whether this person agreed, but what the system does to the ties the
  person is held in: whether it exposes people who were never asked,
  whether it lets a community see what is being inferred about it, and
  whether anybody can contest a classification that describes a group
  rather than a member. None of those are questions a consent checkbox is
  shaped to ask, and none of them are answered by giving one more person
  one more switch.

  The weakness is in the word community. Relational language is at its
  most persuasive and least precise exactly here. Somebody has to decide
  what the community wants, who speaks for it, and who is inside it.
  Those are the questions a government answers for you if you do not
  answer them yourself, and Bernard Matolino and Wenceslaus Kwindingwi's
  objection to Ubuntu as a public ethic is that it has repeatedly been
  answered by whoever already held power.

  So the module ends where it should be hardest to agree. A data regime
  built on relationships, with no account of who speaks for the
  relationship, hands the collective interest to whoever is loudest. The individual rights this view finds too thin are also the thing a
  dissenter holds when the community has decided against them, and a
  teenager whose family would rather a diagnosis stayed inside the house
  is relying on exactly the bounded individual the argument set aside.
  The reconstruction worth having keeps both, and nothing in the
  tradition guarantees that it can.
