one_line: >-
  Of things some are in our power, and others are not.
one_line_attribution: Epictetus, Enchiridion, ch. 1
read: |-
  Stoicism starts by narrowing the question. Epictetus opens the
  Enchiridion by splitting everything into what is in our power and what
  isn't — and puts almost nothing there except one act: giving or
  withholding assent, the judgment you form about what happens to you.
  Chrysippus made the same point with a rolling cylinder, preserved by
  Cicero in De Fato: a push gets it moving, but its own shape decides how
  it rolls afterward. That's the whole doctrine. Everything is caused, and
  responsibility still lands somewhere, because judgment is the one link
  in the chain that's properly called yours.

  Put an algorithm in the picture and the question sharpens rather than
  changes. A hiring system, a loan model, a self-driving car's crash logic
  — none of it was ever up to you. What's up to you is how you judge it
  and what you do next. That isn't resignation. It's where Stoics think
  your actual standing lives: not in outcomes you never controlled, but in
  the one act nothing external can reach.
gets_wrong: |-
  Utilitarians would say this gives up on the world too easily — if only
  your own judgment counts, you have no real argument for building fairer
  systems, only for tolerating unfair ones with good posture. Virtue
  ethicists would say Stoicism treats character as a fortress you retreat
  into rather than something built by acting well among other people, and
  that a private act of judgment isn't the same as a public act of
  justice.
