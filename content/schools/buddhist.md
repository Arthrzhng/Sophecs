read: |-
  The passages Sophecs uses are Pali, from the Theravada canon, and passed
  through long oral and editorial transmission before being written down,
  so they are witnesses to an early tradition rather than transcripts. Damien Keown reads it as a virtue ethics
  organised around nirvana; Charles Goodman as a consequentialism of
  character. Sophecs follows Charles Hallisey and Jay Garfield in
  declining the choice, taking the tradition on its own terms: non-harm, intention, compassion and liberation. Intention is what the
  Western categories keep mislaying: an act is weighed by the volition
  behind it as much as by what follows.

  The discourse to Rahula gives the method. Before acting, while acting, and
  after, you reflect on whether it tends toward your own harm, another's,
  or both, and if it does you stop and say so. That
  is an impact assessment with no end date, an awkward and useful thing to
  hold a deployed model against. The question is not whether the launch
  review passed, but what the thing is doing now and who it is still
  hurting.
gets_wrong: |-
  Damien Keown points out that Buddhist traditions carry a great deal of
  moral teaching and little systematic moral philosophy of the kind the
  West built, the emphasis having fallen on liberation and monastic life
  rather than politics. For
  AI governance that is a real gap. Compassion and non-harm do not tell
  you who owns a dataset, who audits a model, or how somebody appeals a
  decision it made about them. Charles Hallisey
  answers that the missing theory may be context-sensitive reasoning
  rather than a failure to theorise, which makes this a reconstruction
  problem, not a verdict. Utilitarians would press anyway: a school
  weighing intention this heavily lets a well-meaning system off for
  damage a careless one would be condemned for.
