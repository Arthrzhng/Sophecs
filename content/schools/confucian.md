name: Confucian Role Ethics
one_line: >-
  To employ the people as if you were assisting at a great sacrifice.
one_line_attribution: The Analects, 12.2 (trans. James Legge)
status: draft
read: |-
  The Analects was assembled by several hands over time, so what
  follows is the book's position rather than one man's. Sophecs takes it
  as role ethics, the reconstruction Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont argued
  for, rather than as a virtue ethics of cultivated qualities. You are
  not a bearer of general properties who then enters relationships; the
  relationships constitute you, as somebody's child, student, colleague,
  citizen. What you owe is never owed in the abstract. Book 12 sets the
  test for anyone holding power over others: employ them as though
  assisting at a great sacrifice.

  Book 13 supplies the other half. If names are not correct, it says,
  language does not accord with the truth of things, and affairs fail.
  Call a ranking system intelligent, or fair, or explainable, and the
  word has done work the system has not. The Confucian question about an AI
  is not what it maximises. It is which relationships it
  makes, which it degrades, and whether the people inside them can still
  hold each other to what they owe.
gets_wrong: |-
  Reciprocity inside a role leaves the role itself unexamined. The paper
  Confucian Role-Ethics with Non-Domination concedes the point: role
  ethics is too ready to authorise hierarchy, and mutual duties do not by
  themselves make a ruler and a subject equals. Li-Hsiang Lisa Rosenlee's
  Confucianism and Women shows what that cost historically, tracing how
  filial piety and patrilineal descent held women in place. An algorithm
  can honour every expectation attaching to employer and worker and leave
  the power between them exactly where it found it. Utilitarians would
  add that a school built on who owes what to whom has little to say
  about a stranger nobody stands in a relation to.
