read: |-
  Bentham's opening move sounds almost administrative: pleasure and pain
  can be weighed, and the right act is whichever produces the largest net
  sum of good over harm, added across everyone it touches. A unit is a
  unit, whoever feels it. Mill kept the sum but rejected the idea that
  pleasures differ only in amount — some kinds of good, he argued, are
  worth more regardless of quantity, and people who've genuinely tried
  both will tell you so.

  Self-driving cars turned this from theory into hardware. If a crash is
  unavoidable, should the software minimize total casualties, even if
  that means the car chooses its own passenger? Germany's ethics
  commission tried to rule the question out entirely, banning the
  weighing of victims against each other — then quietly left that exact
  question unresolved when the actual law was written. A utilitarian
  doesn't get to dodge it. Somebody has to add up the harm either way, the
  only choice is whether you do it on purpose or by default.
gets_wrong: |-
  Rawls put the sharpest version of this: utilitarianism treats a whole
  society's welfare the way one person treats trade-offs across their own
  life, netting gains against losses as if it were all the same subject
  bearing both. It isn't. The pedestrian who dies so five others live
  isn't compensated by their survival, and no sum makes that trade fair to
  the one person paying for it. Stoics would add that outcomes were never
  yours to guarantee in the first place — the calculus asks you to answer
  for a total no one person actually controls.
