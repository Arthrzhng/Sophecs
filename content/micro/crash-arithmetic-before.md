body: >-
  Jeremy Bentham wrote the procedure down as if it were a recipe. To judge an
  act, take each person affected, add up the pleasures it brings them and the
  pains, do the same for everyone else, and take the balance across "the total
  number or community of individuals concerned" (An Introduction to the
  Principles of Morals and Legislation, 1789, chapter 4). He listed what to
  weigh: intensity, duration, certainty, nearness, and how many people the
  thing touches.


  Nobody had a machine to run it on. Now there is one, and the crash is where
  the recipe stops being abstract. A car that cannot avoid a collision has a
  few hundred milliseconds and a choice between trajectories. One kills the
  passenger. One kills two pedestrians. Bentham's arithmetic is not hard here,
  and the whole difficulty of the motion is that it is not hard.


  Germany's Ethics Commission on automated driving answered in 2017 by
  forbidding the sum: no offsetting of victims against one another, no
  weighting by age or condition. That is a fact about German law, not an
  argument, and the argument is what you are here for. Either the balance
  Bentham described is the right thing for a car to compute, and the passenger
  counts as exactly one, or something in the procedure goes wrong when the
  quantities are people. Say which, and say why.
