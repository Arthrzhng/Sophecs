title: The cylinder and the top

  author: Cicero

body: >-
  The oldest version of this argument is about a cylinder. Chrysippus, head of
  the Stoic school in the third century BC, held that everything happens by
  fate and that we are still responsible for what we do. Asked how both could
  be true, he gave the image Cicero preserves in De Fato 42–43. Push a
  cylinder and it rolls; push a spinning top and it spins. The push is the
  same; what differs is in the object. The push starts the motion, and the
  shape of the motion comes from the thing's own nature.


  For Chrysippus the point was about people. An impression arrives from
  outside and you cannot stop it arriving. Whether you assent to it comes from
  the kind of mind you have, and that is enough, he thought, to make the act
  yours even though the whole chain was fated.


  Now put a language model where the cylinder was. A prompt arrives. The
  output is fixed by weights fixed by training fixed by data nobody chose in
  detail. The chain is at least as determined as anything Chrysippus imagined,
  and he did not think determinism was the problem. So either the model has
  something that plays the part of the cylinder's shape, a nature that gives
  the output its form, or it does not, and the difference between a mind and a
  rolling object was never the push. Decide which before you write, and decide
  what the missing thing would have to be.
