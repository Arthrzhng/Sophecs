title: What Bentham was actually designing
source:
  author: Jeremy Bentham
  work: Panopticon
  section: "Letter V"
body: |-
  The essence of Bentham's design is not the walls but the sightlines. He
  proposed a building in which one inspector, placed at the centre, could
  observe every cell without any occupant knowing whether he was being watched
  at that moment. What mattered was not constant observation — that would be
  impossible — but the apparent omnipresence of the inspector. A person who
  might be watched at any time, he argued, behaves as though watched at all
  times.

  Bentham thought this a humane invention. It replaced the lash with attention.
  The cost of order would fall, the number of keepers would fall, and the
  inmate would be reformed by nothing more violent than the knowledge that
  someone might be looking.

  The building was never completed in England in his lifetime. But the
  principle — that visibility is itself a kind of power, and a cheap one —
  outlived the architecture. A system that directs officers toward particular
  streets does not watch everyone equally. It makes some people more visible
  than others, continuously, and at very little cost to whoever is doing the
  watching.
