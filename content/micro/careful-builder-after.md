title: All punishment is mischief
source:
  author: Jeremy Bentham
  work: An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation
  section: 'chapter 13, §§1–2'
body: >-
  Bentham's objection starts by refusing the idea that blame is something people
  deserve. "All punishment is mischief: all punishment in itself is evil," he
  writes in chapter 13 of An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and
  Legislation. It is justified only where it "promises to exclude some greater
  evil." He then lists the cases where punishment is unwarranted, and one of
  them is where it is inefficacious: where it could not have changed the conduct
  that led to the harm, because the person could not have known or could not
  have done otherwise.


  Blame is punishment's quieter cousin, and the same test applies. Blaming the
  careful builder in proportion to the harm would not undo it, and would not
  have prevented it, since by hypothesis the care was taken. What it would do is
  teach every other builder that the size of the disaster, not the quality of
  the work, decides their fate, and the rational response to that lesson is to
  disclose less and test less anywhere failures are visible.


  So for the utilitarian, proportional blame is bad policy before it is unfair:
  an evil imposed without the greater evil it was supposed to prevent. Blame
  belongs exactly where it changes future conduct, on the shortcut, the skipped
  audit, the warning seen and ignored. A large harm with none of those behind it
  is a tragedy and a reason to redesign, and the builder should be asked what
  they learned, not made to answer for a number.
