title: Right in proportion as they tend

body: >-
  John Stuart Mill's statement of the principle is one sentence, and the
  important word in it is a small one. Actions are right "in proportion as
  they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of
  happiness" (Utilitarianism, chapter 2, 1861). Tend. Mill is not asking
  whether you know why an action works. He is asking what it does, on the
  whole and over time.


  That framing was built for doctors, lawmakers and ordinary people making
  decisions they could at least describe. Now imagine a system that cuts
  hospital readmissions by a fifth, and whose designers cannot say which
  features of a patient's record it is weighing. The tendency is measurable.
  The reasoning is not.


  Two readings are open. On one, Mill has already answered: the tendency is
  the whole of the moral question, and demanding an explanation on top is a
  separate preference, perhaps a reasonable one, but not a moral requirement.
  On the other, "tend" was doing quiet work. A tendency is something you can
  assert only because you understand enough of the mechanism to expect it to
  hold tomorrow, in a new hospital, with different patients. Without that you
  have a track record, and a track record is a different thing.


  Which reading is Mill's is a fair question. Which one you would defend is
  the one you are about to answer.
