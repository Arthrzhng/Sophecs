body: >-
  Elizabeth Anscombe coined the word "consequentialism" in a 1958 paper, and she
  coined it as an accusation. In "Modern Moral Philosophy" (Philosophy 33, no.
  124) she argues that a tradition running from Sidgwick onward had made one
  move older ethics never made: it treated every action, including the killing
  of an innocent person, as something to be weighed against outcomes. Her
  example is the judicial execution of someone known to be innocent to avert a
  disaster. Anyone who thinks it is even an open question whether that should be
  considered, she writes, "shows a corrupt mind."


  That is a hard sentence, and it is aimed at the crash calculation directly.
  The utilitarian rule enters the passenger as a line item before anything has
  happened, written on an ordinary Tuesday by people who will never be in the
  car. Anscombe's claim is that a person of good character does not have a
  number for that. There are things they will not do, and the refusal is not
  squeamishness or a failure to think it through. It is what having a character
  consists of.


  Notice what the objection does not say. It offers no rival calculation. It
  says the calculation was the mistake. A utilitarian answering it has to
  explain why a person willing to compute this is not worse for being willing,
  and "the numbers favour it" is exactly what Anscombe says a corrupt mind would
  reply.
