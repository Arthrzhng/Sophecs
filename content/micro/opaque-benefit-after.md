title: Cleverness is not wisdom
source:
  author: Aristotle
  work: Nicomachean Ethics
  section: 'VI.12, 1144a23–29'
body: >-
  The sharpest objection to deploying a system you cannot explain comes from
  Aristotle, and it turns on a distinction most people never draw. In
  Nicomachean Ethics VI.12 (1144a23–29) he separates practical wisdom,
  phronesis, from something he calls cleverness, deinotes. Cleverness is the
  ability to hit whatever target you have set. If the target is good the
  cleverness is praiseworthy; if the target is bad it is "mere smartness."
  Practical wisdom includes cleverness but is not the same thing, because the
  wise person also sees what the target should be and can say why.


  An opaque optimiser is cleverness in its purest form. It hits the mark. What
  it cannot do, by construction, is give an account of the mark, notice when the
  mark is wrong, or tell a case its training covered from one it did not. On
  Aristotle's view that is the gap between a tool and an agent, and the gap does
  not close because the tool's hit rate is high.


  So the objection is not that explanation would be nice to have. It is that the
  deployers have handed the judgement about the target to something with no
  faculty for judging targets, and kept only the easy part of the decision. A
  long record of hitting the mark says nothing about whether anyone is still
  looking at it. The utilitarian reply has to say why that does not matter, and
  "suffering went down" restates the claim without defending it.
