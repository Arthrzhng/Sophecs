title: The faculty that uses impressions
source:
  author: Epictetus
  work: Discourses
  section: '1.1'
body: >-
  The Stoic reply does not argue about whether the training worked. It argues
  that the training was aimed at the wrong thing. In Discourses 1.1 Epictetus
  asks what, of everything we have, is really in our power, and answers that the
  gods gave us only one thing entirely our own: the faculty of making right use
  of impressions. Not the body, not property, not reputation, not outcomes. What
  is ours is the moment between an impression arriving and our saying yes to it.
  Goodness, for a Stoic, is that moment going well.


  On this view a person who reliably does kind things has not shown they are
  good. They have shown a behaviour. What would show goodness is that they judge
  each impression correctly, assent to the true ones and withhold from the
  false, and that this judging is theirs. Aristotle's habituation and
  human-feedback training both work on the behaviour and hope the inside
  follows. Epictetus says the inside was the only thing ever in question.


  A model has no impressions. It has inputs, and nothing in it examines them and
  decides they are true. So it cannot be good in the sense that matters, and it
  cannot be bad either. It is like a well-made knife: a preferred thing, an
  indifferent, whose value lies entirely in the hands of whoever holds it. The
  motion says the model has become good the way a person does. The Stoic says
  the analogy was flattering to both.
