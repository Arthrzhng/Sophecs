title: He who saves a fellow creature
source:
  author: John Stuart Mill
  work: Utilitarianism
  section: chapter 2
body: >-
  The utilitarian objection to this whole debate is that it is beside the point,
  and Mill made the case with one example. "He who saves a fellow creature from
  drowning does what is morally right, whether his motive be duty, or the hope
  of being paid for his trouble" (Utilitarianism, chapter 2). The motive, Mill
  says, "has nothing to do with the morality of the action, though much with the
  worth of the agent." Two questions, and the one that matters for the act is
  the one about the act.


  Transpose it. A model produces a discharge recommendation, or a loan refusal.
  Whether anything inside the model assented or chose is a question about the
  worth of the agent, and Mill's position is that there may be no agent there to
  have any worth. Fine. The recommendation still sends someone home. The refusal
  still lands on a family. What the output does to people is untouched by the
  metaphysics of how it was generated.


  So the utilitarian says: argue about whether it decides if you enjoy that, but
  do not mistake it for the moral question. The moral question is what the
  outputs do, who is made better or worse off, and who can change the system so
  it does better. The Stoic and the virtue ethicist both have their
  responsibility story pointing at the inside of the box. Mill's points at the
  world, and the world is where the drowning people are.
