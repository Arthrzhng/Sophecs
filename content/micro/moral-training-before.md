title: We become just by doing just acts
source:
  author: Aristotle
  work: Nicomachean Ethics
  section: 'II.1, 1103a31–b2'
body: >-
  Aristotle's account of how anyone becomes good is the closest thing in the
  ancient world to a training procedure. Virtue of character, he says, does not
  arise in us by nature; we are fitted to receive it, and habit completes it.
  The comparison is to a craft. "We become builders by building and lyre-players
  by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by
  doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts" (Nicomachean Ethics II.1,
  1103a31–b2). The order matters. The acts come first. The character that would
  make them come naturally is the result, not the cause.


  Set that beside how a model is trained on human feedback. It produces an
  output. Raters compare it with another and prefer one. The preferred behaviour
  is reinforced and the other suppressed, over millions of rounds, until the
  tendency to produce the preferred kind of thing is, in some sense, built in.
  Repetition first; the settled disposition after. An Aristotelian reading the
  training paper would recognise the shape.


  Whether the shape is the substance is the motion. Aristotle also says, a few
  pages on, that a just act done by accident, or under instruction, or for a
  reward, does not yet make anyone just. Hold that thought. The question you are
  defending or attacking is whether a system that has done the acts, a great
  many times, has done the thing Aristotle was describing, or only the part of
  it that was visible from outside.
