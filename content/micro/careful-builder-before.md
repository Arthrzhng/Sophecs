body: >-
  Bernard Williams begins with a lorry driver. Through no fault of his own, a
  child runs into the road and he kills it. He could not have stopped. Everyone
  agrees he is not to blame, and Williams's observation is that he will still
  feel something the passenger beside him will not, and that we would think less
  of him if he did not. Williams calls it agent-regret. It attaches to what you
  did, not to what you did wrong, and being told it was not your fault does not
  remove it ("Moral Luck," 1976, collected in Moral Luck, 1981).


  His larger claim is that morality cannot be walled off from luck the way
  philosophers since Kant have wanted. Two drivers can be equally careful; one
  kills a child and one gets home. It seems both wrong to blame the unlucky one
  more and false to say nothing about them differs.


  Now the driver is a team of engineers and the lorry is a model that was
  tested, audited and deployed with care, and harmed someone anyway. The motion
  says they are to blame in proportion to the harm. Williams gives you the
  materials for three different answers: that blame tracks fault and the harm is
  irrelevant; that the harm changes what they are answerable for even without
  fault; or that "blame" was the wrong word, and the real question is what an
  engineer who did everything right should feel. Pick one, and be ready to
  explain the driver.
