title: Aristotle on what a practice makes of the people who practise it
source:
  author: Aristotle
  work: Nicomachean Ethics
  section: "II.1"
body: |-
  Aristotle's claim is that the virtues are not given to us by nature but
  acquired by practice. We are not born just or unjust. We become just by doing
  just acts, temperate by doing temperate ones, brave by doing brave ones. The
  things we have to learn before we can do them, he says, we learn by doing.

  The consequence is uncomfortable for any argument that weighs outcomes alone.
  If character is the sediment of repeated action, then what a practice does to
  the people who carry it out is not a side effect to be netted off against the
  benefit — it is the main effect, accumulating slowly and largely unobserved.
  A force habituated to treating certain streets as suspect does not merely
  produce arrests. It produces officers, and eventually a city, formed by that
  habit.

  So Aristotle would press the question the burglary statistics cannot answer:
  not whether fewer houses were entered this year, but what kind of institution
  the practice is making, one shift at a time. A city can reduce crime and
  still, by his measure, be getting worse at being a city.
