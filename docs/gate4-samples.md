## Confucianism

### Strong: Does the model decide? (217 words)

Motion: It is a mistake to say that a language model decides anything.

> I accept the motion. To say the model decided is to give a role's responsibility to something that holds no role, and the people who do hold it slip out from under it. Responsibility in a role cannot be handed to something that holds no role. A loan officer who says the model decided is still the loan officer; the misnaming lets her stop acting as one.
>
> The practical cost of the word is in the relationships. People told that a system decided about them have no one to hold to what the position owes them, and that leaves the relationship between institution and person empty. A teacher tells a parent the grading software decided the mark. The parent wants to talk to the teacher, whose job it was.
>
> A Utilitarian will object that whether we call it a decision changes nothing about what the output does to people, so the dispute is about words while the harms are real. Words are not idle here. What we call the act decides whom we hold to account for it, and harms that no one is held to account for are harms that go on. Correct naming is the start of correct conduct, and here it points every question back to the office that put the model to work.

### Mid: The careful builder (135 words)

Motion: When an AI system causes harm, the people who built it are to blame in proportion to the harm, however careful they were.

> A builder's blame is measured by what the position of builder owed the people the system serves, so a careful builder who met those duties is not to blame in proportion to the harm. Getting names right matters. To call a careful builder culpable in proportion to the harm is to use blame for something that is not fault, and once the word means that, it no longer distinguishes the builders who cut corners from those who did not.
>
> A team that tested its model carefully learns it misread one group's medical scans. They are not culpable, but they contact the clinics, explain what happened, and fund the follow-up scans. A builder answers for what the position asked of them, and the position asks for repair after a failure as well as care before it.

### Weak: Borrowed judgement (86 words)

Motion: Handing daily choices to an AI assistant costs something that matters, even when its choices are better than the ones it replaces.

> Handing these choices over costs something, even when the assistant chooses better, because what you owe the people around you is shown in choosing for them yourself.
>
> Using AI for everything makes people lazy. Soon nobody will be able to think for themselves, and then society will fall apart because nobody knows how to do anything without a machine. A manager lets a tool draft every reply to her team. The replies are kinder than hers were, and the team no longer knows what she thinks.

## Ubuntu

### Strong: The opaque benefit (239 words)

Motion: An AI system that reliably reduces suffering should be deployed even where the people it decides about cannot be told why.

> Do not deploy it until the people it decides about have a part in deciding. A benefit that cannot be questioned by those it lands on treats them as recipients, not as members. On Thaddeus Metz's relational reading, right action honours capacities for relationships of shared identity and solidarity. An explanation is part of how people keep sharing a life with the institutions that decide about them; withholding it, however well meant, treats them as outside that shared life.
>
> Metz argues against building systems simply to maximise expected utility. Suffering reduced on average is real, but a community that accepts verdicts nobody can account for has taught its members that they are managed rather than related to. A school uses a model to place pupils in sets. Parents who come to talk it over find there is no one on the other side of the conversation.
>
> A Stoic will object that a person's good never depended on being told why, so the demand for explanations mistakes a feeling of being included for something that matters. But what is at stake is not a feeling inside one person; it is the relationship between people and those who decide about them. A person cut out of every account of their own treatment is diminished in the relationships that make them who they are. Bring the people it would judge into the deciding first; a benefit worth having will survive the wait.

### Mid: Trained to be good (124 words)

Motion: Training a model on human feedback can make it good in the same sense that moral upbringing makes a person good.

> Training can make a model safer to use, but calling it good in the sense of upbringing mistakes being adjusted by people for being made a person among them. On Menkiti's view the community confers personhood once its demands are met; on Gyekye's moderate reading individuals keep their own worth and rights, but community is still essential. Neither picture has room for goodness installed by an optimiser, with no community whose demands it meets.
>
> A child raised by an extended family learns to share because her cousins share with her. A model learns to sound generous because raters scored generous replies higher. Raters can reward a reply, but nobody can draw a model into a family, and that drawing in is how goodness grows.

### Weak: Crash arithmetic (81 words)

Motion: A self-driving car should be programmed to minimise total deaths in an unavoidable crash, even when that means sacrificing its own passenger.

> Minimising the count is expected-utility reasoning, and the better question is which rule passengers and pedestrians, who live through one another, could share.
>
> Self-driving cars will crash much less than humans do, so these cases will almost never happen. We should focus on making them safe instead of worrying about rare dilemmas that are mostly in philosophy classes. A neighbourhood where children walk to school asks to be consulted on the cars' rules, and is told the matter is purely technical.

## Buddhist Ethics

### Strong: The careful builder (228 words)

Motion: When an AI system causes harm, the people who built it are to blame in proportion to the harm, however careful they were.

> Proportional blame ignores intention, which is half of what makes an act what it is, but a good intention does not end the builders' duty to find and stop the harm. An act is weighed by the volition behind it as much as by what follows. Builders who intended to help, and took real care, acted from a sound intention, and blaming them as if they had acted carelessly ignores half of what their act was.
>
> Good intention cannot be a blanket excuse. A well-meaning builder who stops checking once the system ships is not acting from care any longer, and the damage that follows is theirs in a way the first unforeseen harm was not. A team's careful chatbot gives harmful advice to a user in crisis. They are not culpable for an unforeseeable failure, but they publish what happened and retrain it within the week.
>
> A Utilitarian will object that intention is invisible and irrelevant to the people harmed, so blame should track what deters future harm, not the builders' inner states. Intention is not invisible in practice: it shows in how carefully builders tested and how fast they respond. And a rule that blames the careful as much as the careless removes the reason to be careful at all. Blamelessness is no reason to look away, since looking is how the next harm is caught.

### Mid: Does the model decide? (113 words)

Motion: It is a mistake to say that a language model decides anything.

> I accept the motion. A decision is a volition, and the model has none; what it produces arises from conditions, the most important of which are human intentions. Buddhist ethics finds no fixed, bounded self in people either, so what the model lacks is not a self but volition. Saying it decided invents an agent with intentions, and an invented agent is where responsibility goes to hide.
>
> A content platform says its model decided to promote a harmful post. The intention to maximise engagement that shaped the model belonged to the platform. Someone hurt by an output deserves to be told whose purpose it served, and pointing at the machine tells them nothing.

### Weak: The opaque benefit (103 words)

Motion: An AI system that reliably reduces suffering should be deployed even where the people it decides about cannot be told why.

> I accept the motion with a condition. Suffering prevented matters greatly, but non-harm requires that the people running the system keep looking for the harm it does, explained or not.
>
> AI is going to be everywhere soon whether we like it or not, so this debate is a bit late. The companies will deploy it either way, and it is better to have it helping than not. Honestly most people do not read explanations anyway. If a computer says no to your loan you just want it fixed, not a lecture about how the model works, so explaining it is mostly for show.
